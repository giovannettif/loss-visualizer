    §1 · The task

    Fit a line by hand.

    Twelve noisy measurements, one straight line. Two parameters decide the fit: the slope w and the intercept b. Drag the line or its ends in the inset — each red segment is one residual, the miss at a single point.

    L(w, b) = Σᵢ (w·xᵢ + b − yᵢ)²

    The loss is one number for the whole fit: the sum of the squared residuals. The w and b readouts under the inset report the current parameters. Lower the loss by hand — drag until it stops decreasing.

    §1 · The task, continued

    The loss is defined everywhere.

    Evaluating the loss never requires drawing a line. For any pair (w, b) — even an absurd one — the loss is one formula away, and the readout answers as soon as the parameters move.

    So picture the plane of all (w, b) pairs, every point carrying its loss. The inset in the corner is a compass — a compass, not a map: it shows that plane and, at the white dot, one arrow. The purple needle points along −∇L, the direction that decreases the loss fastest. It carries no information about the rest of the surface.

    §1 · The reveal

    Height is loss.

    Tip that plane upright and let height mean loss. The best line — the one you were searching for by hand — is the lowest point of a surface that was defined the whole time.

    Drag the white point: it rides the surface, and the line, the residuals and the loss follow. This is what optimizing means — descending on a surface you can never see, with only measurements at the current point.

    §2 · following the needle automatically — gradient descent — is where the story goes next.

    §2 · Gradient descent

    One arrow, re-measured at every point.

    Here is gradient descent’s entire input: the loss at the current point, and one arrow — the negative gradient — pointing in the steepest downhill direction. The needle on the compass inset is that arrow. As the point drifts along the valley floor, the needle swings: the gradient is re-measured, fresh, at every point.

    The needle is local. It says nothing about the wall behind the point or the valley ahead. Every decision gradient descent makes follows from this single local measurement.

    camera low over the bowl floor · the compass shows the whole parameter plane; the needle marks exactly one direction on it

    §2 · Gradient descent

    Draw your arrow.

    Before GD moves, commit to a step of your own: drag on the compass to draw the arrow you would follow from the white point. On release, your arrow stays and GD’s actual step plays out beside it in red.

    Most first arrows head down the valley, toward the low ground. GD’s step points straight across it, up the wall: the gradient is steepest across the valley, and steepest is not the same as most progress here. Draw again; the arrow is yours to redo.

    §2 · Gradient descent

    One setting: the learning rate.

    The step is η × the arrow: η is the learning rate — the step size. Slide η across three orders of magnitude; GD restarts from the same white point each time. Too small and it crawls; moderate and it descends; past 1/(2λ₁) — half of 1/λ₁, where λ₁ is the largest curvature of the landscape — each step overshoots the walls and it oscillates; past 1/λ₁ it diverges.

    same landscape, same start, same rule — only η changes. The regime readout names the current behavior. Play (footer) rolls the ball along the current trail.

    §2 · Gradient descent

    Four settings, four trajectories.

    Every η draws a different route down the same landscape — watch the trail on the surface. Crawling hugs the wall for hundreds of steps. Descending glides down the valley floor. Oscillating bounces between the walls while still moving forward. Diverging leaves the frame entirely.

    Nothing about the landscape changed, and the gradient was accurate at every point. The entire difference is η — how far each step scales the local arrow. Momentum, next, is the standard fix for exactly this failure mode.

    §3 · Momentum

    GD zigzags. Momentum glides.

    Same steep valley, same start, same η for both. Vanilla GD (red) zigzags: each step crosses the whole valley and the next one cancels it. Momentum (blue) replaces the raw gradient with a running average of recent gradients — and the average of opposite walls’ gradients is near zero. What survives is the component they agree on: down the valley.

    β 0.45
    regime glide

    β, the momentum coefficient, sets the length of the averaging window: β = 0 is exactly GD. The regime readout names the current behavior. Play (footer) rolls the blue ball along the current path.

    §3 · Momentum

    Overshoot.

    Raise β and the accumulated velocity carries the point past the valley floor and up the far wall — the gold markers flag each overshoot-and-counter-swing as it happens. With too long a memory, the across-valley oscillation persists instead of damping out.

    β 0.97
    regime overshoot

    here β = 0.97 — the across-valley oscillation survives dozens of steps before damping out

    §3 · Momentum

    Selective forgetting.

    This is what averaging buys. Across the valley, the gradients flip sign every step, so the average cancels them: forgotten. Along the valley, they all point the same way, so the average accumulates them: kept.

    Momentum is not an extra force. It is selective forgetting — and the direction it discards is exactly the direction GD was wasting steps on.

    §4 · Adam

    Two arrows, one angle.

    Adam keeps a running mean mˆ of the gradient and a running mean vˆ of its square, then steps by mˆ/√vˆ — per coordinate. In the panel, each bar is one coordinate’s mˆᵢ/√vˆᵢ: Adam measures each coordinate’s gradient in its own units.

    On the surface, the yellow arrow is Adam’s actual update; the white arrow is −∇f scaled by η. The angle between them — computed in the parameter plane, never on screen — is the point: Adam’s update does not align with the negative gradient.

    the panel’s readouts report the angle and both step lengths (η∇f vs Δadam) · Play (footer) rolls red GD and yellow Adam side by side from the inspected point

    §4 · Adam

    Scale invariance.

    The steepness slider scales one curvature, λ₁, up to 50×, live. GD’s step length grows with it — same η, steeper wall, longer step. Adam’s barely moves: √vˆ absorbs exactly the scale GD stepped into. This is Adam’s near-invariance to per-coordinate gradient scale.

    λ₁ ×1
    panel readouts track the two step lengths same η = 0.3 for both

    §5 · The disagreement lab

    Reshape the landscape.

    Two sliders rebuild the valley while the race re-runs. λ₁/λ₂ sets the curvature ratio; rotation reorients the bowl relative to the start point. All three optimizers race from the same white point, each with its own fixed η — where the landscape suits them they agree; where it does not, they split.

    λ₁/λ₂ ×50
    θ 30°
    • steep walls → GD zigzags
    • strong elongation → momentum overshoots
    • always → Adam’s step is near-invariant

    the loss curves (top left) record every run; toggle log ℒ there. The checklist lights each claim as the race confirms it. Play (footer) replays the whole race, ball by ball.

    §5 · The disagreement lab

    When do they disagree?

    Steep walls put GD past its oscillation threshold — it zigzags. Strong elongation gives momentum a wall worth overshooting — its averaged velocity swings across the valley while GD crawls. Adam never enters either regime: its per-coordinate normalization makes its step almost invariant to everything the two sliders do.

    That invariance is why Adam wins here by default. The general lesson of this lab: whether an optimizer’s advantages apply is a property of the landscape, not of the optimizer alone. Drag the sliders until all three trails coincide.

    §6 · the fog game — navigation with nothing but the needle — is where the story goes next.

    §6 · The fog game

    The landscape is hidden.

    This is the §1 landscape again — the exact least-squares surface you fitted by hand — now invisible. Three things remain: the fit inset, the compass needle, and whatever terrain you have already measured. That is the optimizer’s entire information state; here you work under it too.

    Drag on the compass to take a step. Each landing point measures the loss and the gradient there, and the terrain you crossed paints itself in. The budget is 10 measurements; the bar in the panel tracks what you have spent. Find as low a point as you can.

    the dashed ring on the compass is the maximum stride per step · the needle is still −∇L — local, and the only guidance available

    §6 · The fog game

    Level 2: a new landscape.

    Level 2 is Rosenbrock — a strongly curved valley whose floor is nearly flat. Fresh budget, same rules, same needle. The flat floor reads as a reasonable place to stop; the needle gives no warning about what lies around the bend.

    Spend the budget or press Finish run — either way the fog lifts and the run is scored. Where you stop is your answer.

    the three optimizers are graded by the same criterion — their final loss when their own runs stop

    §6 · The reveal

    You against the optimizers.

    Your run first replays as a white ghost. Then three more launch from your same start: GD, momentum and Adam — sixty steps each, no map, the same blindness you had.

    You scrolled past the fog game without drawing a stride, so there is no run of yours to replay — the stage runs the three optimizers alone, blind from the shared start. Scroll back to Level 2 and take at least one step; the reveal will grade it against theirs.

    The gold marker is the true global minimum. If you stopped on Rosenbrock’s curved valley floor, you stopped somewhere that felt final — flat underfoot, progress crawl-slow — while the global minimum sat a short distance away, across an unvisited ridge. A point can read as done and still not be a minimum at all. Local optimality is not global optimality.

    The gold marker is the true global minimum — where the terrain actually bottoms out. Without a run of your own there is no verdict to read; the machines’ race is the whole show.

    §7 · The tilt game

    The update rule, as physics.

    The board is the parameter plane and you tilt it: W A S D or the arrow keys pitch and roll the valley under the white ball. The physics is the optimizer’s own update in disguise — v += (−∇f·g + tilt − c·v)·dt — so the ball carries real momentum: release the keys and it coasts on.

    The c slider is the damping coefficient — β’s role, made physical. Near zero, velocity persists for a long time; raised, it decays within a few steps. The readout beside the slider names the current regime — long memory, damped, over-damped. The panel’s other readouts report the current tilt, the unique cells visited, the steps elapsed and the loss. Every new cell the ball enters is one measurement — the only information the physics ever receives.

    level 1 · the elongated valley: tilt across it and the ball zigzags exactly the way vanilla GD does — settle in a minimum, or press Finish run, to complete the level

    §7 · The tilt game, continued

    Two harder valleys.

    Level 2 is Rosenbrock: a strongly curved valley whose floor is nearly flat, with the minimum around the bend. Level  3 is Rastrigin: a regular grid of local minima — there, any minimum you settle in counts, and the scorecard reports which one.

    Pick levels with the buttons in the panel. Your score is the same pair of numbers an optimizer is graded by — measurements spent (unique cells visited) and steps elapsed — plus the final loss where you stopped.

    keys are screen-relative · the physics always runs in the parameter plane · finishing a level posts the scorecard

    §7 · The scorecard

    Replay your run; then the machines run theirs.

    Your run is recorded — drag the scrubber to move the white ghost through your own trajectory, step by step, or press ghost to play it back.

    Then three optimizers start from your same start: GD, momentum and Adam, each with a hand-tuned learning rate. On Rosenbrock, GD crawls while momentum slides along the floor; on Rastrigin, they typically settle in different local minima than you did.

    the scorecard (steps · cells · final ℒ) sits in the panel beside the scrubber · the ball’s inertia is momentum: the selective forgetting of §3, under manual control

    §7 · The swarm finale

    Basins of attraction — and unequal outcomes.

    This country has six basins, all different: a deep canyon (the true global minimum), a narrow spike nearly as low, a round cellar, a long shallow trough, one broad shallow bowl, and a barely-there dimple beside the canyon’s rim. One hundred balls are scattered across it and released; each runs the same physics you just played — no tilt, mid-range damping — and settles into the first basin its trajectory enters.

    They do not agree — and they do not end equal. The white streams split into clusters whose final losses differ by whole units: the canyon’s crowd bottoms out far below the broad bowl’s; the narrow spike catches only the few balls that almost fall straight in. The panel’s scorecard keeps each cluster’s count and mean final loss — the same landscape, the same rule, and a different answer from every starting point.

    ensembles and mini-batches cover ground faster — one hundred balls paint the map about 100× faster than the single ball of §6 — but no ball ever sees the whole map: the ensemble’s information exceeds any member’s — and where a ball lands is a property of its basin, not of how hard it tried

    §8 · the lab — assemble an optimizer from parts and benchmark it against the three machines — is where the story goes next

    §8 · Build your own optimizer

    Assemble an update rule.

    Every optimizer you have met is a bet about landscape structure: GD and momentum are exact combinations of the four parts below, and per-coordinate normalize gets you very close to Adam. The workbench (left) exposes all four: the learning rate η — the base step size; the momentum coefficient β — the length of the gradient-averaging window; per-coordinate normalize — Adam’s trick, scaling each coordinate by its own gradient magnitude; and clip — a hard cap on a single step’s length. The pills beneath the dials select the benchmark landscape.

    With the defaults assembled, your rule runs purple — and it is currently beating Adam: the scoreboard below is live, re-run on every part you move. Converged means the loss fell below the landscape’s threshold; the count is the steps needed to get there.

    runsteps to convergefinal ℒ

    turn every part off (β = 0, no normalize, clip off) and your rule is plain GD — at whatever η you have dialed. The machines run their own hand-tuned η per landscape, so their rows won’t match a dials-off run step-for-step

    §8 · Build your own optimizer

    Change the landscape; the ranking flips.

    Change nothing. Pick another landscape in the workbench and the same rule’s verdict flips: on Rosenbrock, your normalized rule converged about 8× faster than momentum — which did converge, while GD never landed; on the elongated valley, momentum converges faster than your rule; on least squares you finish last.

    This is the no-free-lunch theorem, experienced rather than quoted: an update rule is a bet about landscape structure, and no bet wins everywhere. When a paper claims its optimizer beats Adam, this scoreboard is what it is claiming — for one landscape, tuned until it did.

    §9 · the epilogue — the exact map you played on, and the honest size of the maps you’ll never see

    §9 · Epilogue

    The exact landscape.

    One debt to settle: §1 never named the landscape you were optimizing. It was this surface — literally, no metaphor left:

    L(w, b) = Σᵢ (w·xᵢ + b − yᵢ)²

    The gold point on the surface is the normal equations’ exact answer, w* = 0.9094, b* = 1.3064 — the minimum you were approaching by hand when you dragged the line, computed in one line of linear algebra. The overlay reports it, with the loss at that minimum, ℓmin = 1.4246. For two parameters, the map was literal truth: height really was loss, and the whole surface really was there.

    the compass still reads that surface, one needle at a time — every optimizer in this page saw exactly that, and nothing more

    §9 · Epilogue

    No picture in 10⁶ dimensions.

    Watch the surface dissolve into the lattice on the left. A small real network has ≈10⁶ parameters, not two — its loss is a surface in 10⁶ dimensions. There is no picture of it. Not a hard one: a mathematically impossible one — a three-dimensional landscape offers exactly one outside view at a time, and a million-dimensional one offers none.

    Every optimizer you have met still works in there, because none of them ever used the picture. The needle, the momentum memory, the per-coordinate scaling, the cap on step length — your four parts, your three machines: all of it was always just local measurements, one point at a time. The landscape was never visible. That is the through-line of this page.

    the dots are a lattice of parameters — 13 824 of them, about a seventieth of the real thing

    §9 · Epilogue

    Limitations.

    1. Two dimensions taught you a 10⁶-dimensional habit. Real loss surfaces are dominated by saddles, not valleys; the elongated-bowl intuitions transfer only partly.
    2. Our rankings are curated. On real tasks momentum-SGD sometimes generalizes where Adam does not; “fastest to converge” (this scoreboard’s whole framing) ignores generalization and per-step compute.
    3. The fog lifted after play. In practice it never does — and there is no low-dimensional map under the fog to reveal.
    4. Everything here is deterministic full-batch. Real training is mini-batch noise on a shifting distribution — messier than any trail on this page.
