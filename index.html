    §1 · The task

    Fit a line by hand.

    Twelve noisy measurements, one straight line. Two parameters decide the fit: the slope w and the intercept b. Drag the line or its ends in the inset — each red segment is one residual, the miss at a single point.

    L(w, b) = 12Σi=1 (w·xi + b − yi)²

    The loss is one number for the whole fit: the sum of the squared residuals. The w and b readouts under the inset report the current parameters. Lower the loss by hand — drag until it stops decreasing.

    §1 · The task, continued

    The loss is defined everywhere.

    Evaluating the loss never requires drawing a line. For any pair (w, b) — even an absurd one — the loss is one formula away, and the readout updates the instant the parameters move.

    So picture the plane of all (w, b) pairs — call the pair θ = (w, b) — every point carrying its loss. The inset in the corner is a compass: it shows that plane from above and, at the white dot, one arrow. The purple needle points along −∇L, the direction that decreases the loss fastest. The faint contour rings are a gift to you alone — the optimizer receives the needle, which says nothing about the rest of the surface.

    §1 · The reveal

    Height is loss.

    Tip that plane upright and let height mean loss. The best line — the one you were searching for by hand — is the lowest point of a surface that was defined the whole time.

    Drag the white point: it rides the surface, and the line, the residuals and the loss follow. This is what optimizing means — descending on a surface you can never see, with only measurements at the current point.

    §2 · next, the machine follows the needle for you: gradient descent.

    §2 · Gradient descent

    One arrow, re-measured at every point.

    Here is gradient descent’s entire input: the loss at the current point, and one arrow — the negative gradient — pointing in the steepest downhill direction. The needle on the compass inset is that arrow. As the point drifts along the valley floor, the needle swings: the gradient is re-measured, fresh, at every point.

    The needle is local. It says nothing about the wall behind the point or the valley ahead. Every decision gradient descent makes follows from this single local measurement.

    From here on, the surfaces are test landscapes: their two coordinates are x and y — still θ = (x, y) — and the loss is still L. The camera rides low over the bowl floor; the compass shows the whole parameter plane, faint contours and all, and the needle — everything GD gets to know — marks exactly one direction on it.

    §2 · Gradient descent

    Draw your arrow.

    Before GD moves, commit to a step of your own: drag on the compass to draw the arrow you would follow from the white point. On release, your arrow stays and GD’s actual step plays out beside it in red.

    Most first arrows head down the valley, toward the low ground. GD’s step points almost straight across it, down the near wall toward the floor: barely any of it goes along the valley. The gradient is steepest across the valley, and steepest is not the same as most progress here. Draw again; the arrow is yours to redo.

    §2 · Gradient descent

    One setting: the learning rate.

    The step is η × the arrow: η is the learning rate — the step size. Slide η across three orders of magnitude; GD restarts from the same white point each time. Too small and it crawls. Moderate and it descends. Past 1λmax, where λmax is the steepest curvature the landscape has, each step overshoots the walls and it oscillates; past 2λmax it diverges outright. On this landscape λmax = 2, so the thresholds sit at η = 0.5 and η = 1: slide past them and watch.

    η 0.0632
    regime descending

    Same landscape, same start, same rule — only η changes. The regime readout names the current behavior. Play (footer) rolls the ball along the current trail, at the speed you pick beside it.

    §2 · Gradient descent

    Four settings, four trajectories.

    Every η draws a different route down the same landscape — watch the trail on the surface. Crawling hugs the wall for hundreds of steps. Descending glides down the valley floor. Oscillating bounces between the walls while still moving forward. Diverging leaves the frame entirely.

    Nothing about the landscape changed, and the gradient was accurate at every point. The entire difference is η — how far each step scales the local arrow. Momentum, next, is the standard fix for exactly this failure mode.

    §3 · Momentum

    GD zigzags. Momentum glides.

    One steep valley, one shared start, one η for both. Vanilla GD (red) zigzags: each step crosses the whole valley and the next one cancels it. Momentum (blue) replaces the raw gradient with a running average of recent gradients — and the average of opposite walls’ gradients is near zero. What survives is the component they agree on: down the valley.

    β 0.45
    regime glide

    β, the momentum coefficient, sets the length of the averaging window: β = 0 is exactly GD. The regime readout names the current behavior. Play (footer) rolls the blue ball along the current path.

    §3 · Momentum

    Too much memory overshoots.

    Raise β and the accumulated velocity carries the point past the valley floor and up the far wall — the gold markers flag each overshoot-and-counter-swing as it happens. With too long a memory, the across-valley oscillation persists instead of damping out.

    β 0.97
    regime overshoot

    The slider arrives at β = 0.97, where the across-valley oscillation survives dozens of steps before damping out. Drag β down and watch the regime readout: below about 0.85 it glides; below about 0.25 it is back to GD’s zigzag.

    §3 · Momentum

    Selective forgetting.

    This is what averaging buys. Across the valley, the gradients flip sign every step, so the average cancels them: forgotten. Along the valley, they all point the same way, so the average accumulates them: kept.

    Momentum is not an extra force. It is selective forgetting — and the direction it discards is exactly the direction GD was wasting steps on.

    §4 · Adam

    Two arrows, one angle.

    Adam keeps a running mean mˆ of the gradient and a running mean vˆ of its square, then steps by mˆ/√vˆ — per coordinate. In the panel, each bar is one coordinate’s mˆᵢ/√vˆᵢ: Adam measures each coordinate’s gradient in its own units.

    On the surface, the yellow arrow is Adam’s actual update; the white arrow is −∇L scaled by η. The angle between them — computed in the parameter plane, never on screen — is the point: Adam’s update does not align with the negative gradient.

    the panel’s readouts report the angle and both step lengths (η∇L vs Δadam) · Play (footer) rolls red GD and yellow Adam side by side from the inspected point

    §4 · Adam

    Scale invariance.

    The steepness slider scales one curvature, λ₁, up to 50×, live. GD’s step length grows with it — same η, steeper wall, longer step. Adam’s barely moves: √vˆ absorbs exactly the scale GD stepped into. This is Adam’s near-invariance to per-coordinate gradient scale.

    λ₁ ×1

    §5 · The disagreement lab

    Reshape the landscape.

    Two sliders in the dock (bottom-left) rebuild the valley while the race re-runs: λ₁/λ₂ sets the curvature ratio; rotation reorients the bowl relative to the start point. All three optimizers race from the same white point, each with its own fixed η — where the landscape suits them they agree; where it does not, they split.

    The loss curves (top left) record every run; toggle log L there. Each claim in the checklist lights up when the race confirms it. Play (footer) replays the whole race, ball by ball.

    §5 · The disagreement lab

    When do they disagree?

    Steep walls put GD past its oscillation threshold — it zigzags. Strong elongation gives momentum a wall worth overshooting — its averaged velocity swings across the valley while GD crawls. Adam never enters either regime: its per-coordinate normalization makes its step almost invariant to everything the two sliders do.

    That invariance makes Adam steady, not fast: it never zigzags or overshoots, yet at this setting it reaches the floor last. The general lesson of this lab: whether an optimizer’s advantages apply is a property of the landscape, not of the optimizer alone. Drag λ₁/λ₂ down to ×1 — a round bowl — and GD and momentum fall onto one straight line while the checklist’s first two lights go dark; Adam’s trail still bends off it, because per-coordinate steps don’t follow the bowl’s shape in either direction.

    §6 · now try it with the surface hidden — navigation with nothing but the needle.

    §6 · The fog game

    The landscape is hidden.

    This is the §1 landscape again — the exact least-squares surface you fitted by hand — now invisible. Three things remain: the fit inset, the compass needle, and whatever terrain you have already measured. That is the optimizer’s entire information state — and for the next two levels, it’s yours too.

    Drag on the compass to take a step. Each landing point measures the loss and the gradient there, and the terrain you crossed paints itself in. The budget is 10 measurements; the bar in the panel tracks what you have spent. Find as low a point as you can.

    The dashed ring on the compass is the maximum stride per step. The needle is still −∇L: local, and the only guidance available. The ticks on the right edge of the screen jump between steps — handy when a level deserves a second attempt.

    §6 · The fog game

    Level 2: a new landscape.

    Level 2 is Rosenbrock — a strongly curved valley whose floor is nearly flat. Fresh budget, same rules, same needle. The flat floor reads as a reasonable place to stop; the needle gives no warning about what lies around the bend.

    Spend the budget or press Finish run — either way the fog lifts and the run is scored. Where you stop is your answer.

    §6 · The reveal

    You against the optimizers.

    Your run first replays as a white ghost. Then three more launch from your same start: GD, momentum and Adam — sixty steps each, no map, the same blindness you had.

    With no measurements spent on this level there is no run of yours to replay — the stage runs the three optimizers alone, blind from the shared start. Spend at least one measurement there and this reveal will grade your run against theirs.

    runmeasurementsfinal loss L

    The gold marker is the true global minimum. If you stopped on Rosenbrock’s curved valley floor, you stopped somewhere that felt final — flat underfoot, progress crawl-slow — while the global minimum sat a short distance away, across an unvisited ridge. A point can read as done and still not be a minimum at all: slow progress is not arrival.

    The gold marker is the true global minimum — where the terrain actually bottoms out. Without a run of your own there is no verdict to read; the machines’ race is the whole show.

    Replay (footer) re-runs all four ghosts. No run ever sees the whole surface — not even after the reveal.

    §7 · The tilt game

    The update rule, as physics.

    The board is the parameter plane and you tilt it: W A S D (or ← →) pitch and roll the valley under the white ball. The physics is the optimizer’s own update in disguise — gravity pulls the ball down-slope, your keys tilt the board, damping bleeds velocity: v += (−g·∇L + a·tilt − c·v)·dt — g is the board’s gravity, a how hard your keys tilt it, c the damping you dial below. So the ball carries real momentum: release the keys and it coasts on.

    The c slider is the damping coefficient — β’s role, made physical. Near zero, velocity persists for a long time; raised, it dies away within a second or so. The readout beside the slider names the current regime — long memory, damped, over-damped. The panel’s other readouts report the current tilt, the unique cells visited, the steps elapsed and the loss. Cells are how the scorecard counts measurements; the physics itself reads the slope under the ball at every step.

    Level 1 is the elongated valley. Tilt across it and the ball swings wall to wall — momentum’s overshoot from §3, not GD’s zigzag — and c sets how fast the swing dies. Let the ball come to rest (the needle goes quiet) or press Finish run to complete the level.

    §7 · The tilt game, continued

    Four harder maps.

    Level 2 is Rosenbrock: a strongly curved valley whose floor is nearly flat, with the minimum around the bend. Level  3 is Rastrigin: a regular grid of local minima — there, any minimum you settle in counts, and the scorecard reports which one. Level 4 is the archipelago: eight island basins of different depths on a map twice as wide, walled off from each other by true ridges — reaching the deep atoll means steering around them, and every optimizer that plays blind lands on the first island it rolls onto. Level 5 is canyon peaks: one long winding valley running past two tall summits, downhill the whole way — if you can keep the ball in the groove.

    Pick levels with the buttons in the panel. Your score is measurements spent — the unique cells visited, counted on the same grid as each optimizer’s path — plus steps elapsed and the final loss where you stopped. Your steps are physics substeps (120 a second); the optimizers’ rows count their update steps (400 each), a different unit, so the scorecard compares cells and loss, not steps.

    The keys tilt the board relative to your screen, but the physics always runs in the parameter plane — with the camera swung around, a key that points “down” can roll the ball to a higher loss. Finishing a level posts the scorecard; ↻ Restart run rewinds the current one.

    §7 · The scorecard

    Replay your run; then the machines run theirs.

    Your run is recorded — drag the scrubber to move the white ghost through your own trajectory, step by step, or press ghost to play it back.

    You reached the scorecard without playing, so the white ghost is a guided demo ride — a hand-steered route through Rosenbrock’s valley, not yours. Drag the scrubber or press ghost to watch it. …and this scorecard grades your own run.

    You haven’t taken a drive yet, so there is no run of yours on the scrubber — nothing there to replay. …and this scorecard will grade your own run.

    Then three optimizers start from your same start: GD, momentum and Adam, each with a hand-tuned learning rate. On Rosenbrock, GD crawls while momentum slides along the floor; on Rastrigin, the blind steppers never leave the pit you all start in — the egg crate traps them. Climb out of it, and your score beats theirs.

    The scorecard (steps · cells · final L) sits in the panel beside the scrubber. The ball’s inertia is momentum: the selective forgetting of §3, under manual control.

    §7 · The swarm finale

    Basins of attraction — and unequal outcomes.

    The pills on the dock (bottom-left) pick the terrain. Multi-well: six named basins of different depths. Multi-well large: nine, over a wider map — from a deep rift to a barely-there scratch. Himmelblau: the classic polynomial whose four pits are all global minima. Archipelago: eight island basins of every depth behind true ridges — the widest map, where the straits decide who ever crosses. One hundred balls are scattered across whichever terrain you picked and released; each runs the same physics you just played — no tilt, mid-range damping — and settles into the first basin its trajectory enters. A ball that ends on open ground is counted as stalled, never as settled — the swarm line in the panel reports both.

    They do not always agree — and they rarely end equal. On the multi-well terrains the white streams split into clusters whose final losses differ by whole units — the deep basin’s crowd bottoms out far below the broad shallow one’s — while Himmelblau’s four clusters all reach the same floor. The archipelago is that story at its sharpest: its ridges wall the basins off, so a ball’s outcome is decided almost entirely by where it was scattered. The panel’s scorecard keeps each cluster’s count and mean final loss; on a tall panel it collapses to the gold global row behind a “+N more” expander. The dock under the map carries the finale’s controls: the terrain pills, the run blind / show map toggle — the swarm enters fogged, and until you untick it the ensemble paints every cell it crosses, building the map blind — and the replay + scrub transport for re-running the flight.

    Ensembles cover ground faster: together the hundred balls paint roughly 20× the ground any one of them crosses alone. But no ball ever sees the whole map — the ensemble’s information exceeds any member’s. And where a ball lands is decided by the basin it enters, not by anything it did along the way.

    §8 · your turn to build one: assemble an optimizer from these four parts.

    §8 · Build your own optimizer

    Assemble an update rule.

    Every optimizer you have met is a bet about landscape structure: GD and momentum are exact combinations of the four parts below, and per-coordinate normalize gets you very close to Adam. The workbench (left) exposes all four: the learning rate η — the base step size; the momentum coefficient β — the length of the gradient-averaging window; per-coordinate normalize — Adam’s trick, scaling each coordinate by its own gradient magnitude; and clip — a hard cap on a single step’s length. The pills beneath the dials select the benchmark landscape.

    With the defaults assembled, your rule runs purple — and on the opening landscape, Rosenbrock, it beats Adam. Don’t take that on faith: the scoreboard below is live, re-run on every part you move and every landscape you pick, and the line under it says who is winning right now. Converged means the loss fell below the landscape’s threshold; the count is the steps needed to get there.

    runsteps to convergefinal L

    turn every part off (β = 0, no normalize, clip off) and your rule is plain GD — at whatever η you have dialed. The machines run their own hand-tuned η per landscape, so their rows won’t match a dials-off run step-for-step. To check that, dial η to a machine’s tuned value. On least squares, η ≈ 0.008 lands on GD’s 11 steps; on Rosenbrock, GD’s tuned η is the dial’s left end (0.001), where your rule crawls exactly like GD

    §8 · Build your own optimizer

    Change the landscape; the ranking flips.

    Change nothing. Pick another landscape in the workbench and the same rule’s verdict flips: on Rosenbrock, your normalized rule converged about 8× faster than momentum — which did converge, while GD never landed; on the elongated valley, momentum converges faster than your rule; on least squares you finish last.

    This is the no-free-lunch theorem, experienced rather than quoted: an update rule is a bet about landscape structure, and no bet wins everywhere. When a paper claims its optimizer beats Adam, this scoreboard is what it is claiming — for one landscape, tuned until it did.

    §9 · the epilogue — the exact map you played on, and the honest size of the maps you’ll never see

    §9 · Epilogue

    The exact landscape.

    One debt to settle: §1 gave you the formula but never its answer. This is the surface you were optimizing — literally, no metaphor left:

    L(w, b) = 12Σi=1 (w·xi + b − yi)²

    The gold point on the surface is the normal equations’ exact answer, w* = 0.9094, b* = 1.3064 — the minimum you were approaching by hand when you dragged the line, computed in one line of linear algebra: w* = (XTX)−1XTy. The overlay reports it, with the loss at that minimum, L* = 1.4246. For two parameters, the map was literal truth: height really was loss, and the whole surface really was there.

    the compass still reads that surface, one needle at a time — every optimizer in this page saw exactly that needle, and nothing more

    §9 · Epilogue

    No picture in 10⁶ dimensions.

    Watch the surface dissolve into the lattice on the left. A small real network has ≈10⁶ parameters, not two — its loss is a surface in 10⁶ dimensions. There is no picture of it. Not a hard one: a mathematically impossible one — a three-dimensional landscape offers exactly one outside view at a time, and a million-dimensional one offers none.

    Every optimizer you have met still works in there, because none of them ever used the picture. The needle, the momentum memory, the per-coordinate scaling, the cap on step length — your four parts, your three machines: all of it was always just local measurements, one point at a time. The landscape was never visible. That is the through-line of this page.

    the dots are a lattice of parameters — 13 824 of them, about a seventieth of the real thing

    §9 · Epilogue

    Limitations.

    1. Two dimensions taught you a 10⁶-dimensional habit. Real loss surfaces are dominated by saddles, not valleys; the elongated-bowl intuitions transfer only partly.
    2. Our rankings are curated. On real tasks momentum-SGD sometimes generalizes where Adam does not; “fastest to converge” (this scoreboard’s whole framing) ignores generalization and per-step compute.
    3. The fog lifted after play. In practice it never does — and there is no low-dimensional map under the fog to reveal.
    4. Everything here is deterministic full-batch. Real training is mini-batch noise on a shifting distribution — messier than any trail on this page.
