# Proposed Visual Explainer: Visualizing Training & Optimizers

## Introduction

**Learning obstacle: what might a learner know already yet still misunderstand about the topic? Try to state the central difficulty in the concept that you aim to address. Describe a specific case where a learner might make an incorrect prediction or find a result surprising. What reasoning might lead them to that prediction, and how would your explainer help them revise it?**

A student coming out of an intro ML course will know the gradient step update rule θ ← θ − η∇f, just as we have learned in CS375, and they will have seen contour plots with optimization trajectories drawn on them. However, two misconceptions can survive those lessons. The first is the belief that optimizers can see the whole landscape. Every picture of a loss surface depicts the terrain and the path together, so it looks like the algorithm knows where it is going — when in reality the picture only exists because someone with global knowledge of the loss landscape drew it afterwards. The algorithm's entire information at any moment is one loss value and one gradient, measured at one point (plus previous measurements, if it keeps them). The second misconception is that the best step points along −∇f, and that a bigger gradient means a bigger *useful* step — an intuition that holds only on gentle, uniformly-shaped landscapes, and fails exactly where real training happens: where the surface is steep in some directions and shallow in others.

Underneath both sits the central difficulty we aim to address: **the optimizer never sees the task — it only sees a landscape, and the landscape's shape depends on choices (like units) that have nothing to do with how well the model fits.** Taken together, the page sets out to answer three questions at once. The first is the topic's core: why does an optimizer's update point somewhere other than the negative gradient? The second is what "finding the minimum" even means under blindness: the needle tells you which way is downhill, never where the bottom of the world is, so a learner who descends perfectly can still end up far from the global minimum. The third is economic: why does real training devour so much data and compute? Because every single step is a measurement, every measurement costs a pass over a batch of data, and a blind learner needs a great many of them.

A concrete case makes the difficulty vivid. Our learner fits a line `y = wx + b` to data recorded in minutes and watches gradient descent converge in a satisfying spiral. We then relabel the input in hours — identical task, identical data, identical learning rate — and rerun. The learner predicts nothing will change ("we only renamed the units"), reasoning that the model, the data, and the algorithm are all untouched. Instead, the loss surface in (w, b) stretches into a long, narrow valley: a tilted rain gutter whose steep walls dominate the gradient at almost every point. The same optimizer now zigzags between the walls or diverges outright. The surprise compounds with Adam, which most learners expect to fix the valley by taking proportionally smaller steps on the steep walls. It does not: Adam takes near-unit steps in every coordinate at once (its update is m̂/√v̂ ≈ ±1 per coordinate), so its update does not even point along −∇f. And in our fog game, a player who faithfully follows the downhill needle every turn reaches a low point and predicts it is *the* lowest point — but the reveal shows the global minimum sitting across a ridge they never visited. Locally sensible steps, globally stranded.

The explainer revises each belief against the learner's own committed prediction rather than against an assertion in a paragraph. The relabel-units interaction shows the bowl visibly deforming as they change units, making landscape shape a consequence they produced; the draw-your-arrow step lets them bet on the steep direction and watch it lose; Adam's per-coordinate bars and the update-versus-gradient angle readout expose the real mechanism; a "normalize features" button rounds the bowl back out and heals gradient descent, turning the reflex "always normalize your inputs" into something they have seen and caused; and the fog-game reveal separates "good local steps" from "good final answer."

## Interactions & Components

**Interaction: what will the learner change and what will they observe? Why should that observation help resolve the difficulty or lead to insight?**

The explainer is a single scrolling page: prose in a side column while the canvas stays pinned, each text block transitioning the canvas into its next state. The through-line across every component: *optimizers are blind; different update rules are different ways of coping with that blindness.* The blindness has three faces, and the page visits each: **spatial** (you only know where you have measured — which is why local minima can strand you far from the global one), **statistical** (each measurement is a sample from the data, so both the map and the needle are estimates), and **economic** (measurements cost data and compute, which is why real training is expensive).

**1. The task → the landscape** *(Figure 1)*. The learner drags w and b to fit a line to noisy points; residuals light up and the loss (sum of squared residuals) updates live. Then the (w, b) loss landscape *materializes from the task* — the map is not arbitrary scenery, it **is** the error. More precisely, the loss is the average error over the data points — an expectation over the data distribution — so the landscape is drawn *by the data*: a different sample of points would trace a slightly different map. That sample-versus-truth gap becomes tangible later, when the lab's noise toggle turns every measurement into a mini-batch estimate. Insight: good parameters = good fit = low ground. This exact landscape (least squares) runs the whole page.

**2. Gradient descent, by hand** *(Figure 2)*. At a point on the visible landscape, the learner draws their own step arrow before GD's is revealed, then compares: their arrow, the negative gradient needle, GD's actual step, and the outcome. A learning-rate slider sweeps crawl → descend → oscillate → diverge. Insight: steps that "feel right" on steep walls are precisely the zigzag, and step size trades wall-bouncing against floor-speed — a dilemma GD cannot escape.

**3. Momentum** *(Figure 3)*. A β slider morphs the trajectory from zigzag to glide to overshoot-and-counter-swing oscillation. Insight: averaging recent gradients *cancels* the across-valley component while preserving the along-valley one — momentum is selective forgetting, not extra force.

**4. Adam, inspected** *(Figure 4)*. Our update inspector draws the negative-gradient arrow and Adam's actual update arrow together with a live angle readout, plus per-coordinate m̂/√v̂ bars. The learner scales one coordinate's curvature and watches GD's step grow while Adam's barely moves. Insight: Adam steps in units of its own recent gradient — near-unit steps per coordinate — which is *why* its update doesn't point along −∇f.

**5. The disagreement lab** *(Figure 5)*. Sliders for feature scale-difference and feature correlation (and a Normalize button) reshape the landscape — the learner is changing *data properties*, not abstract geometry — while all three optimizers race from a shared start. Insight: the same task with the same optimizer changes behavior purely because the landscape changed; disagreement has a findable boundary the learner can explore.

**6. The fog game** *(Figure 6)*. The §1 landscape goes dark. The learner sees only their fit-preview inset and the gradient needle at their position. Turn-based, they draw an arrow, step, and the terrain they touch paints in — every step is a measurement. Goal: minimize loss in few measurements. Every measurement has a price tag: in real training, one measurement means a forward and backward pass over a batch of data, so the scorecard reports steps, measurements, and *data points touched* (steps × batch size) — the explainer's concrete answer to why real training consumes so much data and compute. At the end, the fog lifts, their run replays as a ghost, and GD, momentum, and Adam ghosts launch from the same start; the reveal also shows each runner's distance from the true global minimum, making "found a low point" and "found the lowest point" a measurable distinction rather than a hunch.

**7. The tilt game, levels, and swarm** *(Figures 7–8)*. Real-time: the learner pitch/rolls the board (keys or joystick) and the ball rolls with true inertia — momentum, embodied; friction maps to damping. Levels: elongated valley (feel the zigzag), Rosenbrock, Rastrigin. A replay scrubber and scorecard compare their run to the machines'; the finale launches 100 balls at once, streaming into distinct minima. Insights: initialization matters, basins of attraction are real, and the optimizers' qualitative disagreement is visible at a glance.

**8. The lab: build your own optimizer** *(Figure 9)*. The learner assembles an update rule from parts (learning rate, momentum β, per-coordinate normalization, gradient clipping) and benchmarks it in steps-to-converge against the three, across levels. They can beat Adam on *this* valley — then switch landscapes and watch their rule lose. A noise toggle turns every measurement into a mini-batch estimate — needles jitter, raw gradient descent scatters — and the running averages inside momentum and Adam suddenly earn a second meaning: they are noise smoothers, which is a large part of why adaptive methods shine on real stochastic training. Insight: an update rule is a bet about landscape structure, and no bet wins everywhere.

**9. Epilogue: honesty** *(Figure 10)*. We reveal the exact formula of the §1 landscape (least squares; the normal equation): for two parameters, the map they played on was literal truth. Then a real model has 10⁵–10⁹ parameters — the map can never be drawn. Everything the learner felt — needle-only, fog, zigzag, overshoot — is what training actually is.

Each interaction resolves the difficulty the same way: the learner commits to a prediction *first*, the interface shows the consequence immediately on the same screen, and a mechanism view (arrows, bars, angles, painted maps) explains why — so beliefs revise against experience, not against claims.

## Limitations

**Limitation: what mistaken conclusion might the visualization encourage?**

Five, and we will name them in the explainer itself. First, **"2D intuitions transfer."** All our landscapes are two-dimensional; real loss surfaces have 10⁵–10⁹ dimensions, where saddle regions rather than valleys dominate the geometry, and the clean zigzag story understates how much of real training is spent on plateaus. Second, **"the rankings we show are universal."** On our convex, noise-free levels Adam tends to win and momentum tends to help; on real tasks — with mini-batch noise, non-convexity, and schedules — momentum-SGD sometimes generalizes better than Adam, and no optimizer dominates. Our fog-game trap is also curated to be escapable; real ones need not be. The "steps to converge" scoreboard could further encourage "fastest descent = best optimizer," ignoring generalization and per-step compute cost. Third, **"the fog lifts."** After playing, the map is fully revealed — exactly the thing that never happens in practice. A learner may conclude that enough measurements eventually recover the landscape, when each real measurement costs a mini-batch evaluation and there is no 2D map to recover at all. Fourth, **"training is this clean."** Our trajectories are deterministic full-batch computations; the tidy, reproducible picture overstates how legible real stochastic training is — and the noise toggle lives in the lab, off by default, so the clean picture is the one most learners will see first. Fifth, **"the map is the truth."** Our landscape is the empirical loss of a handful of points — an estimate drawn from one sample, not the population risk a real model trains on. The epilogue addresses all of these explicitly, but a learner skimming only the figures will still absorb the prettier lie — which is precisely why we state it.

## Conclusion

This explainer teaches adaptive optimizers by first making the learner *be* one. Its differentiators from prior art are deliberate: Distill's momentum article and the TensorFlow Playground show fully-visible maps with trajectories; Gradient Golf lets you play on a visible landscape. None let you *feel* the blindness that defines optimization — so our fog game hides the map and charges a measurement for every pixel of it, and our update inspector shows the mechanism (why updates don't point along −∇f) rather than the outcome. And by pricing every step in data and compute, and by drawing the landscape itself from a visible sample of points, the page answers not only "why doesn't the update point down the gradient" but also "why is finding the global minimum genuinely hard" and "why does training cost the data and compute it does." The full experience is a single real-time web page (TypeScript + Canvas, analytic landscapes, no training compute, statically deployable), so every slider responds instantly and every demo can be performed live. We believe a learner who has been the blind ball will never again look at a trajectory-on-contours figure the same way — and will finally understand why we always normalize our inputs.
