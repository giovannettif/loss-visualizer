# You Can't See the Loss — an interactive explorable on adaptive optimizers

## Introduction

For our project we built **"You Can't See the Loss,"** an interactive 3D explorable explanation about adaptive optimizers — gradient descent, gradient descent with momentum, and Adam. The whole page is built around one idea: *an optimizer is blind.* It never sees the loss landscape the way our figures do; at every moment its entire knowledge of the world is one loss value and one gradient, measured at the single point where it stands. Every section is designed to make that blindness felt first and explained second.

The page runs as nine sections: (1) **The Task**, where a loss landscape materializes out of an actual line-fitting problem; (2) **Gradient Descent**, where the learner draws their own steps; (3) **Momentum**; (4) **Adam**, with an update inspector; (5) the **Disagreement Lab**, where sliders reshape the landscape live; (6) the **Fog Game**, where the learner plays the optimizer on a hidden landscape; (7) the **Tilt Game**, where momentum becomes something you can feel in your hands, ending in a 100-ball swarm; (8) a **Build-Your-Own-Optimizer lab**; and (9) an **Epilogue** that connects the toy map back to real, million-parameter models.

## Learning obstacle

A student coming out of an intro ML course — as we have in CS375 — knows the gradient step update rule θ ← θ − η∇f and has seen contour plots with optimization trajectories drawn on them. However, two misconceptions tend to survive those lessons.

The first is the belief that optimizers can see the whole landscape. Every picture of a loss surface depicts the terrain and the path together, so it looks like the algorithm knows where it's going — when in reality the picture only exists because someone with global knowledge of the loss landscape drew it afterwards. The algorithm's entire information at any moment is one loss value and one gradient, measured at one point (plus the previous ones, if it keeps them).

The second is that the best step points along −∇f, and that a bigger gradient means a bigger useful step. That intuition is really only true on round, well-behaved landscapes; it quietly fails on the elongated, rotated, ill-conditioned ones that show up constantly in real training.

The central thing I aim to address is **the gap between the map and the reality** — between the pictures we show learners (whole landscape, path included) and the actual epistemic condition of training (one local measurement at a time). Our figures quietly hand the learner the wrong job description: *steer along the picture.* The real job is: *take one local measurement, update, repeat — knowing nothing else.* Until a learner feels that gap, the optimizers look like arbitrary flavors of the same idea, and a question like "why doesn't Adam's update point along the gradient?" has no satisfying answer.

A specific case where the learner's prediction breaks: before showing anything, we pose a bet. Here is a steep valley wall and a shallow valley floor — which step makes more progress toward the minimum: stepping along the huge gradient (into the wall), or along the tiny gradient (along the floor)? Almost everyone bets on the steep direction, and the reasoning is reasonable: the update rule itself makes the step grow with the gradient, and the algorithm is literally named *steepest descent*. On an elongated, rotated bowl (curvature ratio ~50), that policy is exactly wrong: nearly all of the step length is spent crossing the valley, only to be cancelled on the next step, while the only progress that matters — along the valley floor — is proportional to the learning rate times the *small* eigenvalue. Raising the learning rate to fix the crawl causes divergence instead. A second surprise waits in the fog game: a player who follows the downhill needle faithfully every turn ends the game proud of a low point — and the reveal shows the true global minimum sitting across a ridge they never visited. Following local downhill, always, is not the same as being at the lowest point.

The explainer revises both beliefs against the learner's *own committed predictions*. The draw-your-arrow interaction (§2) makes them place the steep-direction bet with their own hand and watch it lose. The fog game (§6) makes the epistemic constraint visceral: they only ever saw the needle, and the only terrain they know is terrain they walked on. The update inspector (§4) shows the mechanism instead of the outcome: as the landscape gets steeper, the gradient arrow's step grows while Adam's barely moves — because Adam's per-coordinate step m̂/√v̂ saturates near ±1, its update genuinely does not point along −∇f. And the landscape sliders (§5) let them re-test the corrected model by prediction: make the bowl round and all three optimizers agree; elongate and rotate it, and the disagreement returns exactly as the new mental model predicts.

## Interactions & components

Every section follows the same loop: the learner changes one thing, observes the consequence immediately, and the observation targets one specific piece of the misconception. A universal ▶ Play control animates the ball rolling along whatever trajectory currently exists — including one the learner created.

**§1 The task.** The learner drags the parameters (w, b) of a line fitted to twelve noisy points; residual segments light up and a live loss readout updates. Then the landscape *materializes out of the task itself*: the 3D surface is the sum of squared residuals over every (w, b). *Change → observe:* moving the point worsens the fit and raises the ground. *Why it matters:* this ties the whole page together — the landscape is not an arbitrary hilly world; it is the error of an actual task, and "rolling downhill" means "fitting better."

**§2 Gradient descent.** The compass minimap shows the local gradient needle — *"a compass, not a map."* The learner then **draws their own step arrow** on the minimap and releases: their arrow stays, and GD's actual step animates alongside in red, with a ▶ Play roll showing where each choice ends up. A log-scale η slider (0.002 → 2) re-runs GD live through the four regimes — crawling, descending, oscillating, diverging — each labeled. *Why it matters:* the learner commits to the steep-direction bet with their own hand, and the wall-vs-floor dilemma becomes something they *did*, not something they read.

**§3 Momentum.** On a strongly elongated, rotated valley, a β slider (0 → 0.99) re-runs momentum live: zigzag becomes glide becomes overshoot oscillation, with gold markers flagging each overshoot-and-counter-swing. *Observation:* averaging recent gradients cancels the across-valley component and preserves the along-valley one. *Insight:* momentum is selective forgetting, not extra force — and the ball's inertia in the later tilt game makes it physical.

**§4 Adam.** The update inspector draws two tangent arrows at the current point — the negative gradient (white) and Adam's actual update (yellow) — with the angle between them shown in parameter space, plus per-coordinate m̂/√v̂ bars. The learner drags a steepness slider that scales one coordinate's curvature up to 50×: the readouts show the gradient's step growing **×38** while Adam's step stays **×1.0** (1.9% drift). *Why it matters:* "Adam handles steep directions by taking smaller steps there" is falsified by the learner's own slider; the true mechanism — normalization saturates the step size, so the update stops pointing along −∇f — is visible in the bars and the angle.

**§5 The disagreement lab.** Two sliders (elongation ×1→×50, rotation 0→90°) reshape the live 3D valley while all three optimizers race from a shared start; loss curves and a self-lighting "when do they disagree" checklist update from measured trajectory behavior. *Why it matters:* disagreement becomes a parametrized, findable boundary the learner can explore, rather than a fact to memorize.

**§6 The fog game.** Now the learner *is* the model. The landscape goes dark; only the needle and the fit-preview remain. Each turn they draw an arrow, the ball steps, and the terrain it touched **paints in** — a measurement, against a budget. On the Rosenbrock level, a faithful needle-follower lands in the parabolic trap; the reveal lifts the fog, marks the true global minimum across the ridge — *"the global minimum was over there"* — and replays the learner's run as a ghost beside GD, momentum, and Adam ghosts launched from the same start, with a scorecard. *Why it matters:* the first misconception dies experientially. The learner only ever saw the needle; the only terrain they knew was terrain they walked on; and local downhill, followed always, still stranded them.

**§7 The tilt game.** The learner tilts the valley (WASD / arrows) and the ball rolls in real time: tilt is the chosen step direction, the ball's inertia *is* momentum, and the damping slider is β. Three levels (valley, Rosenbrock, Rastrigin), a scrubbable replay, and a scorecard against the machines. The finale releases **100 balls at once**, which stream down the landscape and cluster into distinct minima. *Why it matters:* initialization, basins of attraction, and optimizer-dependent escape from shallow traps are all visible in a single shot — the assignment's "under what conditions do the optimizers disagree?" answered by a picture the learner produced.

**§8 The lab.** The learner assembles their own update rule from parts — learning rate, momentum β, per-coordinate normalize, gradient clip — and benchmarks it in steps-to-converge against the three. On Rosenbrock, our default parts beat Adam (14 vs 164 steps); switching the landscape flips the verdict and the custom rule finishes last. *Why it matters:* an update rule is a bet about landscape structure, and no bet wins everywhere — no-free-lunch as an experience rather than a claim.

**§9 The epilogue.** The page reveals the exact formula of §1's landscape, L(w,b) = Σᵢ(w·xᵢ + b − yᵢ)², with the normal-equation minimum marked on the surface: for two parameters, the map the learner played on was literal truth. Then the map dissolves into a vast point lattice: a real MLP has ~10⁶ parameters — no map exists. *Why it matters:* it names the honest boundary of the whole explainer at the moment the learner is best prepared to hear it.

## Limitations

The visualization risks encouraging at least four mistaken conclusions. **First, low-dimensional intuitions do not transfer.** All our landscapes are two-parameter surfaces; real loss surfaces have 10⁵–10⁹ dimensions, where saddles rather than valleys dominate the geometry, and the clean zigzag story understates how much of real training is spent on saddle plateaus. **Second, the rankings we show are not universal.** Our levels are convex or mildly non-convex, with full-batch deterministic gradients; on real tasks, mini-batch noise changes everything, momentum-SGD sometimes generalizes better than Adam, and our "steps to converge" scoreboard quietly frames *fastest descent* as *best optimizer*, ignoring generalization and per-step compute cost. The lab is designed to inoculate against this, but the scoreboard framing remains a risk. **Third, the fog lifts.** After playing, the map is fully revealed — exactly the thing that never happens in practice. A learner may conclude that enough measurements eventually recover the landscape, when in reality each measurement costs a mini-batch evaluation and there is no low-dimensional map to recover at all. **Fourth, training is cleaner here than in life.** Our trajectories are deterministic and reproducible; real training is stochastic, noisy, and far less legible. The epilogue states all four explicitly, but a learner skimming only the figures will still absorb the prettier lie — which is precisely why we wrote it down.

## Conclusion

"You Can't See the Loss" teaches one thing deeply instead of ten things shallowly: optimizers are blind, and every classic algorithm — the zigzagging honesty of gradient descent, momentum's forgetting, Adam's normalized steps — is a different way of coping with that blindness. The learner does not read this; they bet against it, lose, play it in the fog, feel it in the tilt of a valley, and finally watch their own optimizer rule win on one landscape and lose on another. By the epilogue, the map they were never supposed to have has been given back, examined, and honestly taken away again — which is the truest thing we can say about what it is like to train a real model.
